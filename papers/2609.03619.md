## One-line takeaway

R²-MAD uses debate history to estimate agent reliability and weight peer influence, targeting the tendency of a wrong majority to reinforce its own misconception.

## Research object

- **World or environment:** Repeated multi-agent debate tasks with a shared conversational state.
- **Agents:** Several debating LLMs plus an experience-memory retriever and confidence estimator.
- **Observations and actions:** Agents exchange candidate answers, retrieve evidence from prior debates, and update confidence-weighted contributions.
- **Goals and interaction protocol:** Correct shared misconceptions without simply amplifying the initial majority.

## What the paper does

The method conditions retrieval on the current consensus level, then uses retrieved experiences to estimate per-agent reliability. Those estimates modulate how strongly each peer affects later debate rounds. The authors compare against single-agent and standard multi-agent-debate baselines.

## Evidence and findings

The abstract reports consistent improvements across several benchmarks, but does not enumerate effect sizes or test social-world objectives. The evidence therefore supports a transferable coordination mechanism, not a general claim that confidence estimates are calibrated.

## Reusable patterns

- Make consensus state a retrieval query rather than a post-hoc statistic.
- Keep historical evidence separate from current peer messages.
- Weight influence by evidence-backed reliability instead of vote count alone.

## Limitations and open questions

Debate correctness is easier to score than norm negotiation or relationship repair. Memories can encode correlated mistakes, and confidence weights may create new authority hierarchies without provenance checks.

## Relation to this map

R²-MAD suggests a social-simulation control: agents should remember prior interactions and calibrate peer influence, while the world logs whether consensus reflects independent evidence or correlated memory.
