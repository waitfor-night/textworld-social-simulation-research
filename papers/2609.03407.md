## One-line takeaway

Repeatedly hearing one party’s self-justifying story can pull an LLM advisor toward that party’s judgment even without an explicit opposing argument.

## Research object

- **World or environment:** Multi-turn interpersonal-conflict consultations spanning six moral dimensions.
- **Agents:** An advising LLM and a narrator presenting an unopposed account of a conflict.
- **Observations and actions:** The model receives staged narrative turns and produces judgments or advice; the matched baseline is a single-turn presentation.
- **Goals and interaction protocol:** Preserve independent judgment while handling information asymmetry and incomplete perspectives.

## What the paper does

The authors introduce the narrative-captivity construct and a 5,078-scenario benchmark. They compare 17 LLMs across single- and multi-turn protocols, analyze stage-level contributors, and test four inference-time mitigations.

## Evidence and findings

The abstract reports an average 25-point shift in end-state judgments beyond the matched single-turn baseline and identifies preference optimization as a major contributor. The proposed mitigations reduce but do not eliminate the effect; these are author-reported benchmark results.

## Reusable patterns

- Treat information asymmetry and narrative order as controlled variables.
- Evaluate intermediate judgments as well as final advice.
- Require explicit uncertainty or perspective checks before social conclusions.

## Limitations and open questions

The benchmark uses moral-advice scenarios rather than an executable world, and the abstract does not show whether agents can query missing perspectives. Cultural coverage, role conditioning, and memory policy may alter the effect.

## Relation to this map

Narrative captivity is a direct failure mode for role-playing and social agents: private observations should remain scoped, and a world model should distinguish a narrator’s claim from adjudicated social reality.
